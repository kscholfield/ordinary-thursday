Not Competing for the Same Prize

Keith ScholfieldNovember 20266 min read

At the recent Business Success Summit, the host asked me a question I hadn't given much thought to.

How do you keep from comparing yourself to people who have so much more?

It's a reasonable question. The room was filled with successful entrepreneurs, some of whom had built businesses worth tens or even hundreds of millions of dollars. We heard stories of extraordinary exits, ambitious growth plans, and people pursuing levels of wealth far beyond anything Shirley and I have achieved.

I don't recall exactly how I answered. But after thinking about it, I keep coming back to something simple: Our life is pretty incredible as it is.

I genuinely don't feel diminished by someone else's greater financial success. I don't know exactly when that changed. I suspect there was a time when I compared myself more readily to people who had accomplished more or accumulated greater wealth. But if so, it's well in the past.

And I think I'm beginning to understand why.

Would You Trade Lives?

There's an old thought experiment about gathering a group of people and asking whether they'd exchange their lives and problems for someone else's. The story usually ends with most people choosing to keep their own.

I don't know whether that's actually true, but the question is useful.

We tend to compare individual pieces of our lives with individual pieces of someone else's. Their wealth. Their home. Their accomplishments. Their freedom.

But we don't get to select just the pieces we admire.

Would I trade my entire life for that of someone worth $50 million? Their marriage, health, family relationships, obligations, worries, and how they spend their days?

Almost certainly not.

That's not a judgment about their life. They may be extraordinarily happy. It's simply a recognition that I like mine.

Shirley and I have been traveling extensively since selling our home and leaving our business lives behind. Again and again, usually in the middle of some remarkable experience, one of us will look at the other and say, "I can't believe this is our life!"

It's not a gratitude exercise. It's astonishment.

And that may be part of the answer. It's difficult to envy someone else's circumstances when you're astonished by your own.

I'll Be Happy When…

A recent mastermind discussion prompted me to consider a familiar question:

I'll be happy when…

For much of my business life, I could have finished that sentence easily. There was always another goal, another opportunity, another problem to solve.

Today, I can't seem to finish it.

More money? It wouldn't fundamentally change our lives.

A bigger, better house? I don't even know where I want to live.

More success or recognition? Neither holds much appeal anymore.

More travel? We're already free to go almost anywhere we want.

I still have ambitions. There are places I want to explore, experiences I want to have, and things I want to accomplish. But I don't feel that my life is missing something while I wait for them.

That's a remarkable change from the way I lived for much of my adult life.

The Price of More

For many years, I wanted to build a successful business.

And we did.

Our Montessori schools provided financial rewards, challenges, relationships, opportunities to create something valuable, and the satisfaction of building a successful organization.

I enjoyed much of that experience. I'm proud of what we accomplished.

But somewhere along the way, what I wanted began to change.

As our wealth grew, the financial benefits of continuing became less consequential. At the same time, I became increasingly aware of what the business required from us.

Time. Attention. Energy. Responsibility. Life force.

I've told Shirley many times that it would take a considerable increase in wealth to materially change our lives, and we didn't have a realistic path to that kind of increase.

We already had the resources to live extraordinarily well.

What we didn't have was unlimited time.

I recently considered a hypothetical offer: Would I work another five years for an additional $10 million?

In 2021, I probably would have. We were planning to continue working until approximately 2025 anyway. Another five years would have fit reasonably well with our expectations.

Today? Not a chance.

Not for $10 million. Not for $50 million.

What would I buy that I don't already have? How would it materially improve our lives?

And what would I be trading away?

Five years of traveling with Shirley while we're healthy and energetic. Five years of family, exploration, writing, learning, and the freedom to decide how to spend our days.

Those years aren't available for purchase later.

That doesn't mean more money has no value. It means the tradeoff has changed.

Not Competing for the Same Prize

At the summit, one participant talked about building his business to $300 million in revenue.

My immediate reaction wasn't envy. I was impressed.

I've known him for years and know how important the business is to him and his family. I also recognize that I don't know all his circumstances or motivations.

And I can admire his ambition without wanting to pursue the same thing myself.

One of the advisors at the summit enjoys his work so much that he jokes about hoping to die at his desk.

Good for him.

I also admire the young entrepreneurs who build businesses and sell them for $50 million. They hit it out of the park. Their accomplishments are impressive.

I'm happy for them.

None of this means I've lost my ambition. I still want to learn, create, explore, and challenge myself. And I can imagine opportunities that might draw me back into building something meaningful.

But I'd be choosing those opportunities because of how I wanted to spend my time, not because I needed another financial accomplishment.

I don't believe my way of living is the best way. There are many paths to a fulfilling life, and mine is only one.

For someone still building the financial resources to live the life they want, pursuing greater wealth may be exactly the right choice. For someone who genuinely loves running a business, continuing to build may be its own reward.

I can admire both without wanting either for myself.

For Shirley and me, Enough meant recognizing that accumulating more wealth was no longer necessary to support the life we wanted.

Once we understood that, the question changed.

Not How much more could we build?

But What do we want to do with the time we have?

I don't know exactly what the next twenty years will look like. I expect our interests will change, new opportunities will appear, and we'll discover things we can't yet imagine.

For now, though, I find myself somewhere in the world with Shirley, doing something we once only imagined we'd have the freedom to do.

And again and again, we look at each other, smile, and say:

I can't believe this is our life!

I hope we never stop noticing.
