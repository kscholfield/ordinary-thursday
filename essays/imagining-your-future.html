Imagining Your Future

Keith Scholfield November 14, 2026 7 min read

At the Business Success Summit, one of the advisors asked whether I could have imagined living the life Shirley and I are living now.

I told him I never could have.

Later, on stage, he mentioned our conversation and put the question to the audience. His response was, “Why not?”

I've been thinking about my answer.

Before we sold the business, I imagined something fairly familiar: our existing life, with the work removed. Enough money to maintain our lifestyle, more control over our time, and some additional travel. I could picture going places we'd always wanted to visit. I could picture having fewer demands on our days.

What I couldn't picture was how different life might feel.

For most of our adult lives, responsibility had exerted a steady mental pull. There were schools to run, employees to support, decisions to make, and problems that might surface at any time. Even when nothing required immediate attention, some part of our attention remained attached to the business.

We took vacations. We could have a relaxing afternoon or a spa day. But the obligations remained. Eventually we would return to them, and we knew it.

We had never experienced life with those weights removed for good. I had no real reference point for imagining it.

Learning what a good day could be

Just after the sale, we spent time in Wailea. I later wrote about it in “Learning to Exhale.”

For most of our adult lives, we had measured a good day by what we accomplished. In Maui, we began asking different questions. Did we move our bodies? Eat fresh, simple food? Spend time together? Notice the beauty around us? Were we fully present?

That was enough. Or, as I wrote then, we were slowly discovering that it was enough.

There was room to float in the ocean without looking at the time, listen to the birds, grill fresh fish, and watch another sunset. We could enjoy being exactly where we were.

I left with a glimpse of a life I had never imagined. At the time, it was only a glimpse. Understanding what had changed took longer.

In July, in Portugal, Shirley and I were relaxing beside the pool at São Lourenço do Barrocal. We had nodded off the previous day, and I asked whether she could have relaxed enough to nap a few years earlier.

“No way.” There had always been too much going on in her head.

That evening we sat outside with a beer and a glass of wine. A warm breeze was blowing, the sun was setting, and the birds were settling down for the night. Shirley looked around and said, “I've never been this comfortable.”

In my notes, I wondered whether our nervous systems were recalibrating. A few days later, in Lagos, I realized she no longer had the Sunday Scaries.

How would we have imagined that absence beforehand? We knew the feeling of Sunday evening and the approaching week. We had much less understanding of how Sunday could feel when the familiar apprehension simply didn't arrive.

My chemistry background gives me an image for it: atoms in a material moving more vigorously at a higher temperature, then settling into gentler motion as it cools. That is roughly how the change feels to me. We are still active, curious, and engaged. There is simply less agitation underneath everything.

I could imagine having fewer things on the calendar. I couldn't imagine how it would feel inside my own head.

Beyond “we'll travel more”

The travel itself has surprised me, too.

In August, the day after watching a total solar eclipse from our ship, we walked part of the Camino Portugués in Pontevedra, then returned to Vigo for lunch. We ordered Galician octopus, prawns, bread, local beer, and Albariño. Shirley's wine was €2.80 a glass. The whole bill was €57.

Our financial advisor emailed while we were eating to ask whether we were having a good time. I replied that perhaps we were having too much of a good time.

Yesterday, an eclipse. That morning, a little of the Camino. Now this wonderful lunch together. It was Thursday, and I couldn't quite believe what an ordinary Thursday looked like now.

I had imagined traveling. I had never imagined that day.

Today, three months later, I boarded a flight from Honolulu to Los Angeles and settled into first class. Los Angeles would be a one-day stopover before we continued to Australia and New Zealand for four months. I opened my laptop to look at places to stay in Switzerland the following summer.

Earlier, I had been looking at rentals on Maui and the Big Island, thinking we might spend a month there before next year's Business Success Summit. Somewhere in between, I texted a friend to ask when she planned to be in Austria. Perhaps we could meet before or after our summer cruise in the Azores.

Then it hit me.

Who the fuck gets to live like this?

Before we sold, I could have listed countries I wanted to visit. I never would have described that afternoon: leaving Hawaii, heading toward four months Down Under, considering Switzerland and Austria because we felt like it, with other journeys already ahead.

This is the life we are exploring in this season. Someone else might find their greatest satisfaction close to home, deeply involved in their community, or continuing work they love. The surprising possibilities in their future may look nothing like ours. But I'd bet they'll discover freedoms they hadn't imagined, too.

Preparing without knowing everything

I understand the value of imagining a future before making a major change. It helps to consider what matters, what enough would make possible, and how we want to spend our days. If we have a partner, we need to hear their answers, too.

But I am less convinced that we can expect ourselves to have a complete picture.

In conversations with owners considering an exit, I hear concern about what comes afterward. I understand it. A business provides much more than income: structure, challenge, relationships, and a familiar place to feel useful. Its absence can be disorienting. Freedom still needs some direction.

Yet feeling that we need a fully formed vision creates its own problem. We may be asking ourselves to describe an experience for which we have no reference point. The picture we construct from inside the business may leave out some of the most meaningful possibilities.

I wouldn't tell someone to assume that leaving will solve everything. I would encourage them to prepare, experiment, and preserve room to learn. Understand what the business supplies besides money. Talk with your partner about the life you each hope for. Try some of it where you can. Be thoughtful about commitments that could consume the freedom you're trying to create.

We don't have to cement a new identity too quickly. We can allow for the possibility that our current vision is incomplete.

An incomplete picture can still give you a direction. You can know that you want more time together, better health, greater flexibility, or room to explore without knowing precisely what those things will become.

Some understanding arrives only after the circumstances change. Some preferences become clearer when there is enough quiet to notice them. And some possibilities emerge because one experience leads to another.

Wailea gave us a glimpse. Portugal revealed a comfort we hadn't known. Vigo showed us what an ordinary Thursday could become. On the flight from Honolulu, I was still discovering how much larger our life had grown than the one I had pictured.

That is my answer to “Why not?”

I had imagined a future using what I knew. Living it has given me more to imagine.
