Learning to Exhale

Keith ScholfieldSeptember 20253 min read

Looking back, I don't think we went to Maui to recover from selling the business.

I think we went there to learn how to breathe again.

We stayed for two weeks at Wailea Ekahi, overlooking Keawakapu Beach. We had visited Maui before, but this time was different. We weren't squeezing a vacation into a busy life. There was no business waiting for us when we got home. No school calendar. No employees depending on us. No unfinished projects quietly demanding attention.

For the first time in decades, there was nowhere else we needed to be.

The memories that stay with me aren't the dramatic ones. They're surprisingly ordinary.

Every day we wandered down the hill to Keawakapu Beach, perhaps my favorite beach in the world. Andrea had introduced us to it years earlier, and it had everything we didn't know we were looking for. The water was warm. The waves were gentle. There was plenty of room to spread out. In September, it sometimes felt as though the beach belonged almost entirely to us.

Shirley would simply soak it all in.

I carried a pool noodle into the water so I could completely let go, floating without effort while looking up at the blue Hawaiian sky. We'd drift in the warm ocean for long stretches, saying very little, listening to the gentle wash of the water.

There wasn't much happening.

And somehow, that was everything.

Later we'd stroll the length of the beach toward the neighboring condos, never in a hurry, listening to the surf and watching the afternoon light change across the sand. Looking back, I don't remember where we were walking to. I only remember how it felt to walk without needing a destination.

Most evenings we bought fresh fish and grilled it on the community barbecue near our condo. We'd carry our dinner back to the lanai, watch the sun sink into the Pacific, and eat outside while the birds filled the air with constant chatter.

One evening Shirley changed the greeting on her old business phone. When we listened to the recording afterward, we laughed because the birds were louder than her voice. She never changed it again. Even today, if you call that number, you're transported back to those evenings in Wailea. Before Shirley says a word, you hear Maui.

Those birds have become an unexpected time capsule.

Our favorite lunch was usually much simpler. We'd head into Kihei to the South Maui Fish Company, eat some of the freshest fish we've ever had, then wander without any particular plan. Everything felt easy.

I also remember how comfortable Shirley became.

She stopped worrying about making herself up before leaving the condo. She'd pull on comfortable clothes, walk down the hill to the beach, and simply be herself. Looking back now, I realize that wasn't just about Maui. It was about what had quietly disappeared. The tension. The responsibility. The feeling that she always had to be "on."

Her nervous system was finally beginning to trust that this new life was real.

Mine was too.

At the time, I thought we were taking a well-earned vacation.

Now I see something entirely different.

We were practicing a new way of living.

For most of our adult lives, we measured a good day by what we accomplished. In Wailea, we began measuring a good day by something else entirely.

Did we move our bodies?

Did we eat fresh, simple food?

Did we spend time together?

Did we notice the beauty around us?

Were we fully present?

That was enough.

Or perhaps more accurately, we were slowly discovering that it was enough.

I left Maui with something far more valuable than a tan or a collection of photographs.

I left with a glimpse of a life I had never imagined.

Not a life of constant luxury or endless travel.

A life with enough margin to float in the ocean without looking at the time.

Enough quiet to hear the birds.

Enough freedom to grill fresh fish, watch another sunset, and realize there was nowhere else in the world we needed to be.
