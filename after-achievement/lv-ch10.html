The Long View

Chapter 10

Regret as Information

Keith Scholfield

The Thing We're Taught to Avoid

Most of the advice about regret is about avoiding it.

Live without regrets. No regrets. The phrase is everywhere — on motivational posters, in retirement toasts, in the implicit promise of a life well-planned. The idea seems to be that regret is a sign of failure: if you make good decisions, you won't have to look back with regret at the ones you made.

I don't think that's right. And I think the effort to avoid regret — to preemptively outrun it — is one of the reasons it tends to accumulate quietly rather than being examined and used.

Regret is information. It reveals something real about the gap between what we intended and what we actually did — between the values we claimed to hold and the choices we actually made when the moment arrived. That gap is often more honest than anything we'd volunteer about ourselves.

What Regret Actually Signals

The regrets worth paying attention to aren't the ones about bad luck or circumstances outside your control. Those are disappointments, which are different.

The useful regrets are about choices. The pattern is consistent: people tend to regret inactions more than actions, deferred priorities more than pursued ones, and the things they never tried more than the things they tried and failed. The accumulation of small safe choices — each one reasonable, each one defensible — often produces more regret than a single bold decision that didn't work out.

This has practical implications. It means that the long view of a life is more likely to be shaped by what you chose not to pursue than by the outcomes of what you did pursue. The experiences deferred indefinitely. The relationships allowed to drift. The conversations avoided because the timing never felt right.

The Tradeoffs That Compound

Every meaningful choice involves a tradeoff. Time spent one way cannot be spent another. Attention given to one thing is attention withheld from something else. Commitments taken on carry opportunity costs that are rarely fully visible at the moment of commitment.

The challenge is that most of the tradeoffs that shape a life aren't made explicitly. They accumulate through small, reasonable decisions made over time — each one justified in isolation, yet collectively producing a direction that was never quite chosen. The business owner who consistently chooses one more year of building over the experiences waiting on the other side doesn't make that choice once. They make it thousands of times in small daily decisions that never feel like choices because the alternative isn't yet pressing.

The regret, when it comes, tends to arrive as a recognition rather than an event. A realization that the window for something important is smaller than it once was. That the years spent on something survivable produced a cost that only becomes visible from the other side.

I know this from experience. The last school story in Chapter 3 isn't a story about a dramatic mistake. It's a story about accumulated small choices — each one reasonable, each one made with good intentions — that collectively produced a cost that wasn't fully visible until the window had already narrowed.

Reading Regret Constructively

The useful response to regret is neither dwelling in it nor dismissing it. It's reading it.

Regret, examined honestly, tends to point clearly toward what actually matters. Not what you think should matter, not what others expect to matter, but what actually matters to you — revealed by the gap between what you did and what you wish you had done.

The useful move isn't pretending things were fine. It's using the discomfort to clarify what you'd do differently given another chance. That doesn't require self-flagellation. It requires honesty.

The question isn't "how do I avoid feeling this?" It's "what does this feeling reveal about what I actually value?" And then: "how does that change what I do next?"

The Tradeoffs Worth Making

Not all tradeoffs are regrettable. Some are unavoidable, some are genuinely worthwhile, and some produce outcomes that couldn't have been predicted at the time.

Shirley and I talk occasionally about what we'd do differently. There's an honest answer and a performative one, and the performative one — "no regrets, everything brought us here" — is the kind of thing that sounds wise but isn't really true.

The honest answer is that we'd make some choices differently, and we'd make others exactly the same. Building the schools, even knowing the difficulty, gave us something that the money couldn't have given us — the experience of having built something that mattered, that shaped children's lives, that ran on values we believed in. The last school, knowing what we know now, we wouldn't have built.

The difference between those two isn't the difficulty. It's the alignment. The first schools were genuinely worth what they cost. The last one wasn't.

That distinction — between tradeoffs that are worth making and tradeoffs that aren't — is what the long view is ultimately for. Not to prevent all regret, which is impossible. But to make the tradeoffs consciously, with open eyes, so that the ones you make are at least the ones you actually chose.

Preparation as Care

Regret becomes useful only when it changes what we do next.

It is not enough to notice what we would do differently. The long view asks us to act before hindsight becomes the only teacher.

Some regrets can still be corrected directly. A conversation can be had. A relationship can be repaired. A commitment can be ended. A priority can be moved forward while there is still time.

But other regrets are prevented less dramatically. They are prevented through preparation: documents organized before someone needs them, conversations held before a crisis forces them, explanations that reduce confusion, systems simplified before they become burdens, heirs helped to understand not only what they may receive but why it exists and what it is meant to support.

This kind of work rarely feels urgent. That is why it is so easy to delay. No one wants to spend a healthy afternoon organizing passwords, reviewing estate documents, explaining advisor relationships, or clarifying intentions in a trust. The work feels administrative, premature, and vaguely uncomfortable.

But the cost of not doing it is real. And the cost is usually paid by someone else.

That is one of the quiet lessons regret teaches: many future burdens are optional, but only if they are addressed before they become someone else's emergency.

The long view asks us to treat preparation not as paperwork, but as care.
