The Cape

Keith Scholfield July 2026 4 min read

Recently I stood at Cabo da Roca, the westernmost point of mainland Europe.

A few months earlier I had stood at the Cape of Good Hope, where sailors once celebrated reaching the southern tip of Africa before turning east toward India. Looking out over the cliffs, I found myself wondering what makes a cape different from any other stretch of coastline.

A cape isn't a destination.

It is a transition.

It is a piece of land that extends into the sea, a place where a voyage leaves one body of water and enters another. For centuries, mariners measured their progress not by the ports they visited but by the capes they rounded. Each one marked a change in weather, currents, winds, and possibilities. The voyage after the cape would not be the same as the voyage before it.

The cape itself was never the point.

No one sailed thousands of miles simply to arrive at the Cape of Good Hope. They sailed there because of what came next.

I've begun to think our lives have capes as well.

Graduations. Marriages. The birth of a child. Selling a business. The death of a parent. Retirement. A difficult diagnosis. Moving away from the house where your children grew up. These moments matter because they change the waters we sail. We celebrate them, photograph them, and tell stories about them.

But they are not the destination.

They are places where the voyage changes.

Too often we mistake reaching the cape for completing the journey. We spend decades climbing toward financial independence, retirement, or some other long-awaited milestone, believing that once we arrive we will have arrived.

Yet every meaningful achievement is only another cape to be rounded.

The question is never, Have I reached the cape?

The better question is, Who must I become after I round it?

When Shirley and I sold our business and later our home, it felt as though we had rounded one of life's great capes. The familiar shoreline slowly slipped behind us. We were no longer navigating by deadlines, payrolls, school calendars, or mortgage payments. The waters ahead required different skills, different questions, and a different compass.

That is the hidden gift of a cape.

It reminds us that the charts which brought us this far may no longer be the ones we need.

There comes a moment when the achievements that carried us through one season no longer provide direction for the next. We need new bearings. New questions. New reasons to keep sailing.

Once you've rounded a cape, there is little value in longing for the shoreline behind you. Your attention belongs to the sea ahead.

Every life has its capes.

Each one asks the same quiet question:

Now that your voyage has changed, how will you sail?
