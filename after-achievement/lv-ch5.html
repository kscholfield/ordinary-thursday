Chapter 5

Choosing Your Pillars

Keith Scholfield

Pillars, Routines, and Anchors

After selling the business, I didn't expect letting go of work to be the hard part. I'd done it before in smaller ways, and I thought I was ready.

What I didn't anticipate was how quickly our life would become almost entirely mobile—and how few of my existing structures were designed for that. I had assumed we'd travel in shorter arcs and return home to familiar routines, much like we had in the past. Instead, we found ourselves moving continuously, with no stable rhythm waiting on the other side. Travel stopped being a break from routine and became the routine itself.

That changed the problem entirely.

Most people who have spent time thinking about how they live have some version of a pillar framework—life categories scored on a wheel, domains ranked by how well they're being tended, areas of focus defined in a workshop or a book. The concept isn't new. What I hadn't thought carefully about until this season is a distinction that turns out to matter quite a bit: the difference between the pillar itself and the practices that express it.

Pillars are the permanent domains—the categories of what matters that don't change regardless of what life looks like. They aren't goals. Goals are directional; they aim toward completion. Pillars are stabilizing; they support continuity. In the first game, you could often run on goals because the structure around you was maintaining the pillars passively. Work imposed a schedule that enforced health by necessity. Relationships were sustained through shared life infrastructure. The pillar was always there; it just didn't require active tending.

When that structure disappears, the passive enforcement disappears with it. The second game doesn't introduce the concept of pillars. It reveals which ones were being maintained by default—and which ones now have to be chosen deliberately.

Routines are the full expressions of each pillar when context and stability allow—the specific practices built over years, sometimes decades. Many people who exit a demanding career carry their routines largely intact. The gym doesn't change. The morning coffee and reading. The weekly call with family. These habits were often built around the demands of the first game, but many survive the transition well. There's no reason to dismantle what's working.

Anchors are something different—and they only become necessary when routines aren't available. For those who engineer a significant lifestyle shift after exit, the old routines often can't make the journey. They were tied to a place, a schedule, a physical infrastructure that no longer exists. What I needed wasn't better routines. It was something smaller and more portable: a minimum viable practice that could keep each pillar alive regardless of conditions.

Health

This is the most obvious pillar and the easiest to neglect when structure disappears.

In the first game, health fit around everything else. Work dictated the schedule. Training happened when time allowed. But over time—and this became very clear during our first extended stretch of travel—health isn't something to be managed around life. It's the foundation that allows everything else.

The challenge comes with disruption. On the road, I no longer assume access to a familiar gym, preferred equipment, or a reliable time window. What I've found is that the anchor here isn't the workout itself—it's the decision to train. Frequency and identity, not optimization. Some days that means a crowded hotel gym. Other days it means walking, bodyweight work, or doing less than planned. The standard shifts from "am I training the way I want to?" to "am I someone who trains?"

Without health as a non-negotiable anchor, everything else becomes negotiable in ways that compound quietly.

Relationships

In the absence of constant work demands, relationships take on a different role. They are no longer something to be fit into the margins. They become central.

This is especially true in a long marriage. Time together increases dramatically. Space opens up. And with that comes a different kind of attention—one that the busyness of the first game made easy to defer.

Shirley and I have had to figure out things about each other that more than two decades of shared purpose had kept conveniently obscured. For her, travel is energizing—the novelty, the constant new input, the freedom from domestic routine. For me, there's also a need for space to decompress, for quiet, for time that isn't organized around the next thing. Neither is right or wrong. But both need to be recognized.

This pillar isn't about scheduling quality time or being intentional in a performative sense. It's simpler than that—it's about paying attention. Noticing when the other person is running low on something. Noticing when you are. Building the habit of checking in as a practice rather than waiting until there's a problem.

Wealth

In the first game, wealth was a problem of accumulation. Build more. Earn more. Reinvest. The direction was clear and the measure was obvious.

In the second game, the problem changes entirely. Accumulation is no longer the goal. Stewardship is. The question shifts from how do I build this? to how do I manage what I have, allocate it wisely, and make sure it serves the life I'm trying to build—without becoming its own full-time job?

What has helped is replacing vague vigilance with a small number of clear decisions. Knowing the actual number—what this life costs, what rate of spending is sustainable, what margin exists—is more calming than any amount of general financial diligence. Precision settles what anxiety can't.

Legacy

Without the pressure to produce, thinking takes on a different quality. It slows down. Becomes more reflective. Less about solving immediate problems and more about understanding patterns, connections, and meaning.

Writing has become the primary way I work through this—not as output, but as clarification. The daily notes I've kept while traveling have become something I didn't expect: a record of how experience actually changes a person, in real time.

But the deeper question isn't personal. It's what gets transmitted.

Legacy isn't what happens after you're gone. It's what you actively build while you're here—what you choose to pass on, to teach, to put into a form that outlasts the conversation. The books. The letters. The frameworks kept and refined over years. The conversations with our daughters that go deeper than logistics. These are all forms of contribution that the first game had no time for and the second game makes possible.

Energy and Attention

Of all the pillars, this one surprised me most.

In the first game, energy was spent without much thought. There was always something that required it, so it was given. The feedback loop was short: you spent energy, something got done, you recovered and spent more. The work imposed its own rhythm, and attention followed automatically.

In the second game, both disappear as defaults. And without them, I've started to notice things I never tracked before: what gives energy back, what drains it without obvious return, what activities leave me feeling better than when I started versus which leave me vaguely depleted even when they were technically enjoyable.

Attention is the companion problem—and in some ways the more important one. Energy is the fuel; attention is how you allocate it. Left to default, attention fragments: pulled by the trivial, the immediately stimulating, the path of least resistance. Protected deliberately, it goes toward what actually matters.

What protects both is space. Unscheduled time. Uncommitted days. Room to think without agenda.

The Point

What I've found—and am still finding—is that when these pillars are in place, life regains structure without rigidity. Travel can expand or contract. Work can increase or decrease. Commitments can be added or removed. But the pillars remain. They provide continuity across changing environments. A way to stay grounded when everything else is in motion.

There's also a practical use for this framework that goes beyond personal structure. Every significant decision asks you to reallocate something—time, money, energy, attention. The pillars make that tradeoff visible. Saying yes to a new commitment isn't just an addition to the calendar. It's a draw on one of these accounts. Naming which one—and what it means for the others—is often enough to make the decision obvious.

The most important shift is this: structure is no longer something that happens by default. It's something that has to be chosen. Not perfectly. Not permanently. But intentionally.

Freedom creates space.

Anchors determine what fills it.
