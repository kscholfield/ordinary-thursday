The Long View

Chapter 12

What Holds

Keith Scholfield

Below the Surface

Walking through Barcelona, you move through time without noticing it at first.

A market that feels alive and permanent. A cathedral that has stood for centuries. Streets filled with people going about their lives as if things have always been this way and always will be.

Then you descend below the surface and everything shifts.

What you find is a market — not a symbol of one, but the actual excavated remains of a place that was, in its time, exactly what thriving businesses are today. The center of commerce. Full of activity. Deeply important to the people inside it. Wine makers. Textile merchants. Craftsmen. People building what mattered, in their time, as fully as we build what matters now.

What struck me wasn't how old the ruins were. It was that they were once normal.

Busy. Necessary. Alive with activity and meaning. And then gone — buried, forgotten, invisible for centuries until a construction crew in the 1930s broke through accidentally. No one chose to preserve them. No one even knew they were there.

A short walk away, the Barcelona Cathedral has stood continuously since the 14th century. Not by chance. By the deliberate choice of generation after generation who understood what they had inherited and decided it was worth keeping.

The ruins survived by accident. The cathedral survived by intention.

This is the part that's easy to miss. What disappears rarely feels like it's disappearing while it's happening. It feels stable. It feels permanent. It feels like the way things are.

The Pattern That Repeats

I've seen versions of this pattern in multiple places now, at multiple scales.

In my hometown, land that once held military bases is now filled with housing, parks, and shopping centers. Industrial areas have become residential. Orange groves and open space absorbed into development. Each change felt incremental. Reasonable. Even necessary. But over time, the landscape became unrecognizable. What was once obvious disappeared without ceremony.

In business, the same dynamic plays out. For years, the work feels central. The problems are real. The decisions matter. The identity forms around it. Then one day, the business is sold, and the thing that once occupied so much space in life simply stops. No collapse. No dramatic ending. Just absence. The systems that required your involvement no longer do.

In families, it happens across generations. The values, stories, and practices that seem self-evident to one generation become invisible to the next if no one takes responsibility for transmitting them. Not through deliberate neglect — through the same quiet erosion that buries Roman streets. Life moves on, new layers accumulate, and what was foundational gradually disappears from view.

What Actually Endures

The ruins in Barcelona, and the cathedral standing intact nearby, planted a question I've carried since: why do some things endure and others disappear?

Buildings do not endure simply because they are built well. Many well-built structures disappear — abandoned, replaced, covered over by what comes next. Institutions do not endure simply because they are old. Many old institutions vanish without ceremony. Families do not endure simply because they are wealthy. The shirtsleeves pattern is old and consistent.

What tends to endure is not merely what is built. It is what is cared for long enough, explained clearly enough, and valued deeply enough that those who come after choose to carry it forward.

That distinction matters.

While we are alive, we can maintain things. We can maintain a relationship, a rhythm, a document system, a value, a trust structure, a story, a practice, a home, a way of gathering, or a way of thinking. Maintenance matters. Without it, important things decay quickly.

But maintenance during our lifetime is not the same as endurance beyond it.

For something to endure after us, it must become transferable. It must be made understandable. Stories must be told. Values must be explained. Systems must be simplified enough to be used. Intentions must be made clear. The next generation must be able to see not only what exists, but why it exists and what it was meant to serve.

Even then, transfer is not the final step. The final step is adoption.

Those who come after must decide that what we maintained and explained is worth carrying forward in their own lives. They may carry it differently than we imagined. They may reinterpret it. They may preserve the principle while changing the form. They may choose only part of it. And some things we hoped would endure may not.

That is humbling. It also clarifies the work.

The goal is not to force continuity. That rarely works, and when it does, it can become control disguised as legacy. The goal is to make continuity possible — by tending what matters while we are here, making it transferable, and giving those who come after enough context to choose it freely.

The cathedral endures not because its stones are superior, but because generation after generation of people decided that it was worth keeping. The family endures not because its assets are large, but because its members continue to invest in the human and intellectual capital that allows those assets to serve their intended purpose.

This is the deeper meaning of stewardship. Not just managing what you have. Not just preserving what you built. But taking responsibility for continuity while recognizing that true endurance ultimately depends on those who come after you.

We can maintain. We can transmit. But what endures is what others choose to carry forward.

The Challenge Is That They Often Look the Same

The hardest insight from all of this is also the simplest: some things are built for a moment. Others are built to endure. The challenge is that, in the moment, they often feel the same.

The business that is worth selling and the business worth building another decade — both feel urgent and meaningful while you're inside them. The relationship worth sustained investment and the one that will naturally fade — both feel important in the present. The practice worth maintaining across decades and the one that was appropriate for a season — both feel like parts of who you are while you're doing them.

The long view is the practice of making that distinction more carefully — before the moment has passed and the pattern is visible only in retrospect.

What in my life is built for a moment? What is built to endure? Which of my relationships deserve the active investment that endurance requires? Which of my practices are foundational versus seasonal? Which of the values I want to pass to my daughters are genuinely worth their sustained attention, and which are artifacts of a context that will shift?

These questions don't have clean answers. But asking them regularly — and being honest about what the answers reveal — is the work of someone who has moved from Builder to Steward, and is beginning to think like an Elder.

Invisible Foundations

Those ruins were invisible for centuries. Not because they were unimportant — they were foundational to the city that grew above them. But because the people living above them had no reason to look down. Life had simply moved on.

Much of what we are doing now — the conversations with our daughters, the preparation of documents, the transmission of values, the sustained investment in relationships — is foundational work that will be largely invisible while we're doing it. It won't produce dramatic outcomes or measurable results. It will simply be part of what supports the next layer of lives that grows above it.

That is not a small thing. It is, in many ways, the most important thing.

What you leave behind is not what is engraved in stone monuments. It is what is woven into the lives of others.
