The Long View

Chapter 6

Attention Without Agenda

Keith Scholfield

The Thing That Doesn't Expand With Time

One of the surprises of the second game is discovering that more time doesn't automatically mean more presence.

Time expands. The calendar clears. The urgent demands that once organized every hour recede. And yet—at least in my experience—the quality of attention doesn't automatically improve just because its competition has decreased. If anything, the absence of external structure can make attention harder to direct, not easier. When nothing is urgent, everything feels vaguely available, and the mind moves toward novelty and stimulation as readily as it ever did.

Attention is finite in a way that time is not. Two people can spend the same afternoon in the same place and have entirely different experiences based on where their attention rests. One notices the quality of light, the rhythm of a street, the expression on a face. The other moves through the same environment but inhabits it only partially—half-present, half-elsewhere, capturing photos of an experience they aren't fully having.

I've been both people. Sometimes in the same hour.

The Performance Habit

One of the subtler shifts I've noticed while traveling is a loosening of the need to perform the experience.

At home, performance shows up everywhere—productivity, optimization, even leisure. There's a quiet pressure to extract value from time: to do the right workout, read the right book, choose the best restaurant, make the moment count. When we travel, that habit comes along. The instinct that an experience should be maximized—that I should feel awe, gratitude, or transformation on demand. When it doesn't happen, the disappointment is subtle but real.

What's been freeing is realizing that nothing needs to be proven. A walk doesn't need to become a highlight. A day doesn't need a story. A place doesn't owe me meaning. Some moments are simply neutral—and that's not failure.

Letting go of performance creates space for something quieter: attention without agenda. Curiosity without urgency. Presence without judgment. Ironically, this is when meaning tends to appear—not as a peak experience, but as residue. The things that linger are rarely the moments I tried to make significant. They're the ones I was simply present for.

For more than a decade, our annual travels to Hawaii followed a familiar pattern. We attended the Business Success Summit—a gathering we valued and looked forward to—and we stayed at high-end resorts. Places that matched the register of the event and the identity that came with it. We enjoyed ourselves. We kept coming back.

After selling the business, we returned to Maui but made different choices. A townhouse in Wailea with no concierge. A casual resort in Hana. A bed and breakfast in Makawao. The events were the same. The island was the same. But something shifted.

Hana and Makawao felt closer to the body. Days were shaped by weather and light more than plans. Early bedtimes. Muddy trails. Shirley walking down to the beach in a bathing suit without a second thought. Nothing asked to be dressed up or narrated.

When we returned to the resort for the Summit, we noticed something we hadn't before. Not dissatisfaction exactly, but effort. A subtle reintroduction of pacing, polish, and presentation—small decisions about timing and appearance that we'd barely registered in years past. The resort wasn't worse than it had ever been. But the performance expectation, invisible when we were embedded in the first game, was now visible.

The simpler accommodations hadn't been more comfortable. They had been more honest. And once the difference was clear, it was hard to miss.

Living well, we're learning, often looks less refined from the outside than it feels from the inside.

What Noticing Actually Requires

A few weeks after our first cruise, a friend asked us the natural questions: what did you do, what did you like, what was your favorite experience?

We struggled to answer.

We had spent weeks on a remarkable trip through places most people never see. And we couldn't describe it. The experiences had moved through us without leaving much behind. We'd been present for the events but absent from them at the same time—moving through ports and excursions and dinners without the kind of attention that converts experience into memory, or memory into meaning.

That conversation was a bright line. Before it, I hadn't thought much about how I was paying attention. After it, I couldn't think about much else.

What followed wasn't a formal system. It was a set of small practices that accumulated over time into something I didn't expect.

The first was capture. I started writing things down—not summaries of what we did, but what I noticed. What surprised me. What I couldn't explain. What lingered after everything else faded. These weren't polished essays. They were field notes. Raw observations not intended for something greater, simply a record that we were paying attention.

The second was conversation. Shirley and I started talking through the day—not as a debrief, but organically, over dinner or on a walk. What caught your eye? Why do you think the cathedral is so well maintained when the ruins were left buried? Did that seem strange to you too? She notices different things than I do, and those differences surfaced observations I would have otherwise lost.

The third was review. Periodically—not on a fixed schedule, but regularly enough—I'd go back through the notes and look for patterns. This is where things got interesting.

Reading back through early entries from Barcelona, I found something unexpected: a visit to the Roman ruins beneath the Gothic Quarter followed by a visit to the Cathedral had generated more questions than I'd realized at the time. Why do some things endure while others disappear? What makes an institution carry forward while another crumbles? There was a straight line from that one afternoon, and a question Shirley asked standing in front of a cathedral, to what eventually became the chapter in this book about legacy.

That connection wasn't visible in the moment. It only surfaced in review.

Once you start deliberately noticing, your brain begins scanning for relevance automatically. Everything starts talking to everything else. A conversation connects to something read months earlier. An observation from Hana sheds light on a question from Barcelona. Patterns emerge that wouldn't exist if you weren't looking for them.

Attention Does Not Add to Life

The most useful reframing I've found is this: attention does not add more to life. It reveals more within it.

The same afternoon walk through an unfamiliar city is a completely different experience depending on whether you're moving through it with your phone in your pocket and your eyes open, or with your phone in your hand and your attention split. The walk doesn't change. The experience does.

This matters especially in the second game, when the experiences themselves are often remarkable—new places, extended time, freedom that most people never get. It would be a strange waste to move through that freedom at partial attention, consuming experiences without inhabiting them, collecting stamps rather than understanding places.

The long view of a life includes not just what you did, but how fully you were there for it.
